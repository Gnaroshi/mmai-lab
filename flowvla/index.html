FlowVLA

Enabling World Dynamics Understanding
through Pluggable 3D Flow Learning

Junhyeong GoMingyu JungWoobin ImJongbin Ryu

MMAI Lab · Ajou University

Paper ↗Video ▷BibTeX

Teaching VLA policies how the 3D scene moves, with no additional inference computation.

Overview

A vision-language-action (VLA) policy turns camera images, a language instruction, and the robot’s state into actions. Yet the 2D image-text representations used by many VLAs do not directly describe how a scene changes during manipulation. Hanging a mug, for example, requires the robot to move its handle into alignment with the rack as the interaction unfolds.

FlowVLA uses 3D motion reconstructed from RGB demonstrations as an additional training signal. It links the movement of scene points to the robot’s own movement, so the policy learns about 3D dynamics alongside its action task. At deployment, it uses the original VLA inputs and action decoder. Evaluations on Seer and π₀.₅ show improved manipulation success, robustness, and data efficiency without additional inference computation.

Method

FlowVLA connects two kinds of motion already present in a demonstration: how the observed scene changes and how the robot moves. It uses that connection to train the VLA’s internal action representation through two auxiliary prediction heads.

Recover motion from RGB. VDPM reconstructs trajectories that follow the same 3D scene points over time. Their frame-to-frame displacements define 3D flow. We express these movements in the robot-base coordinate system, then select points with the largest average motion to focus supervision on dynamic regions.

Connect scene motion to control. The point-level head predicts the selected points’ future displacements. The TCP-level head learns how to weight and combine those predictions to match the movement of the Tool-Center-Point (TCP), the reference point on the robot’s end-effector. These complementary objectives link scene dynamics to robot motion.

Keep deployment unchanged. Both flow losses supplement the original action loss during training. At deployment, the auxiliary heads and reconstructed 3D inputs are removed: the policy uses its original observations and action decoder, with no additional depth input or inference computation.

Simulation Experiments

We attach FlowVLA to Seer and π₀.₅, which use different action-generation architectures. LIBERO measures manipulation across four task suites. LIBERO-Plus tests the trained policies under unseen camera, robot, language, lighting, background, noise, and layout changes, without additional fine-tuning.

Data Efficiency and Computational Cost

Training with reconstructed 3D motion also improves success when demonstrations are limited. The auxiliary heads add 0.34% parameters in the reported Seer setting; policy training takes approximately 1.26× as long, with no additional inference computation.

Offline reconstruction is a separate, one-time preparation step: approximately 4 hours for LIBERO-Long, compared with approximately 13 hours of policy training in the reported setup. Its outputs can be cached and reused.

Real-World Experiments

We evaluate Seer with and without FlowVLA on a UR5e robot using third-person and wrist-view observations. Each task uses 40 demonstrations for training and 15 evaluation trials per policy. The seven tasks cover extended manipulation sequences and precise 3D alignment.

Base Tasks

Four long-horizon tasks require the robot to complete a sequence of object interactions.

  • FruitPut the green apple in the pink bowl and put the orange in the green bowl
  • Cup & PlushPick up the white cup and place it on top of the upside-down pink cup, then pick up the blue penguin plush toy and put it in the white cup
  • Stack CupsStack the orange cup on top of the blue cup, then stack the yellow cup on top of the orange cup
  • CabinetOpen the drawer, take the orange cup out and put it on the table, then put the blue cup in the drawer and close the drawer

3D-aware Tasks

Three tasks require accurate alignment between the end-effector, manipulated object, and target.

  • BasketballPick up the red ball and place it in the basketball hoop
  • Hang CupPick up the white mug and hang it on the wooden mug rack
  • Ring InsertionPut the blue ring on the wooden stand, then put the pink ring on the wooden stand

Success across seven tasks

FlowVLA improves success on all seven tasks. Hang Cup increases from 7/15 to 13/15 successes; Stack Cups reaches 14/15. The comparison videos below show individual rollouts alongside the baseline.

Qualitative Results

The visualizations below examine what the training signals capture: which scene points move, how their predicted motion relates to the end-effector, and which points receive the strongest control-related weights.

Selecting motion-salient 3D points

Reconstruction follows scene points across the demonstration. Magnitude-based Top-K selection then retains the points with the largest average displacement. In these examples, selected points concentrate around moving regions while much of the static scene is excluded. The reported Seer configuration uses 2,048 points, without semantic masks or contact labels.

RGB observations and selected 3D points from the same demonstration.

From point motion to TCP trajectories

The predicted trajectories follow the recorded end-effector motion across these examples, illustrating the connection learned by the TCP aggregation head. Black shows the ground-truth TCP trajectory and red shows the prediction. This is a visualization of the auxiliary training branch; deployment uses the original action decoder.

TCP-Level Aggregation

Motion alone does not identify which points matter most for control. The learned aggregation weights emphasize points around the end-effector and object–robot interactions. The four synchronized views connect the RGB demonstration, motion-selected points, high-weight points, and predicted TCP trajectory.

Limitations

FlowVLA needs offline 3D reconstruction, and supervision quality depends on the reconstructed trajectories. Motion magnitude provides a simple way to select active regions, but it does not guarantee that every selected point is relevant to the task. Here, world dynamics refers to manipulation-relevant 3D scene motion; the experiments do not establish general understanding of forces or contact mechanics.
